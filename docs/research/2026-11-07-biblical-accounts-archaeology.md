# Biblical accounts and the archaeological record

Date: 2026-11-07.

Status: research notes for this repository. These pages are not imported into `content/` JSON, and they are not shown in the app. Do not bulk-promote any row here to `scholar-approved`.

Method: two cross-checked research passes. Each claim below was kept only when an edition, museum record, or excavation report supported the exact statement. A name, place, or event that matches a passage is corroboration of that point. It is not proof of a speech, a number, a miracle, or the theology of the book.

Wikipedia is an orienting locator. Where a claim below still cites a Wikipedia URL, the prose also names the edition or museum object. Do not cite the Wikipedia URL alone if this note is later turned into an app citation. The repository build rejects a claim whose only citation is Wikipedia (`learnings/2026-07-19-concrete-sourcing-no-wikipedia-alone.md`).

## How to grade a link

| Grade | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Direct | An inscription or excavated text names the person, place, or event. |
| Consistent | The setting, office, or practice matches. The object does not name the biblical item. |
| Disputed | Specialists still divide on the reading or the identification. |
| Not corroborated | The site sequence or the primary text does not support the account, or no source cleared review. |

Silence is weak evidence against a small highland group. It is strong evidence when a text claims a burned, walled city at a mound that was empty.

## Late Bronze Age

### Merneptah Stele, Cairo JE 31408

Flinders Petrie found it in 1896 in Merneptah’s mortuary temple at Thebes, cut on the rough back of a reused stele of Amenhotep III. Wilhelm Spiegelberg’s copy and translation are in *Zeitschrift für Ägyptische Sprache* 34 (1896) 1–25. Petrie’s *Six Temples at Thebes* (1897) is later. The text is a hymn of year 5. The closing lines name Ashkelon, Gezer, and Yanoam as places, then Israel with the people-determinative: Israel is laid waste, its seed is not.

That is the earliest widely accepted mention of Israel outside the Bible. It shows a group Egypt called Israel in Canaan in the late 13th century BCE. It does not describe an exodus, a wilderness, or a conquest.

The checker accepted the findspot, museum number, year-5 date, line 27, determinatives, and the majority reading. It rejected only a claim that Petrie 1897 was the first publication.

### Berlin pedestal ÄM 21687

Not an earlier “Israel.” The fragment was bought in 1913 and has no findspot. A 2010 restoration by van der Veen, Theis, and Görg (*Journal of Ancient Egyptian Interconnections* 2.4) would read the broken third name-ring as Israel. James Hoffmeier and Robert K. Ritner’s 2020 refutation rejects that reading. It is not evidence for the patriarchs or an early Exodus.

### Amarna letters

About 382 cuneiform tablets from Akhetaten, no more than about thirty years under Amenhotep III and Akhenaten, mid-14th century BCE. Most are in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, the British Museum, and the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. Standard edition: Knudtzon, *Die El-Amarna-Tafeln* (1907, 1915). English: Moran, *The Amarna Letters* (1992). Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem (EA 285–290) and other vassals ask for help against the Habiru. Likeness of that word to “Hebrew” is debated. The letters are Late Bronze politics. They do not identify the Habiru with the Hebrews of Genesis 14 or with Joshua.

### Ai (et-Tell)

Most archaeologists identify the mound with biblical Ai. Judith Marquet-Krause (1933–1935) and Joseph Callaway (ASOR, 1964–1970) found an Early Bronze destruction around 2400 BCE, then a gap until an unfortified Iron I village of about 1200–1050 BCE, settled without a conquest burn and left without a burn. Summaries: *Anchor Bible Dictionary* (1992) and *The New Encyclopedia of Archaeological Excavations in the Holy Land* (1993). There was no Late Bronze walled city for a 15th- or 13th-century attack to destroy, which is what Joshua 7–8 and Joshua 8:28 describe. Alternative sites, including Khirbet el-Maqatir, were not treated as overturning that sequence.

### Jericho (Tell es-Sultan)

Kathleen Kenyon’s 1950s excavation is the baseline: the famous city walls belong to the Middle Bronze Age, and she did not find a Late Bronze fortified city that matches Joshua 6. *Digging Up Jericho* (1957) publishes the 1952–1956 seasons, not a 1958 season. A 2026 abstract by Lorenzo Nigro, seen only as an abstract, argues for a smaller Late Bronze town ending in the early 13th century and still says that phase is not Joshua 6. Kenyon’s “no city” picture should not be overstated. Joshua 6 still has no archaeological corroboration from the reports reviewed here.

## Iron Age II

### Tel Dan stele

Basalt, found in secondary use at Tel Dan in 1993–94, Israel Museum. Avraham Biran and Joseph Naveh, *Israel Exploration Journal* 43 (1993) and 45 (1995). Generally dated to the 9th century BCE (about 870–750). A majority read *bytdwd* as “House of David.” That is a dynastic name set beside the Judahite house in Samuel–Kings. It is not a record from David’s lifetime. The partly legible names Jehoram and Ahaziah are evenly divided. Restored lines, including “seventy kings,” are not secure. Lemaire reads “two kings” rather than seventy. The fragment join is disputed.

### Mesha Stele

Louvre AO 5066, KAI 181. Basalt from Dhiban, engraved shortly after 842 BCE, about 840. It names Mesha of Moab, Omri, Israel, and YHWH. That is the earliest certain extrabiblical mention of YHWH, and it is direct corroboration, with differences, of the Moab–Israel war in 2 Kings 3:4–27. A “House of David” reading of line 31 is disputed. Lemaire’s *bt[d]wd* is contested, including a 2019 “Balak” proposal by Finkelstein, Na’aman, and Römer (*Tel Aviv* 46.1). The Louvre bibliography also lists Richelle’s 2021 re-examination.

### Kurkh stela of Shalmaneser III

British Museum ME 118884, from Üçtepe, found in 1861, inscribed about 852 BCE. It lists “Ahab the Israelite” (*A-ha-ab-bu Sir-ila-a-a*) among the western kings at Qarqar in 853 BCE. Identification with Ahab of 1 Kings 16:29 and 1 Kings 22 is the consensus, and this is the only Assyrian use of “Israel” rather than “House of Omri.” The stela’s “2,000 chariots and 10,000 soldiers” is not a headcount. Na’aman’s scribal-error proposal of 200 chariots is not a consensus.

### Black Obelisk

British Museum ME 118885, black limestone from Nimrud, found by Layard in 1846, made 827–824 BCE. The caption records tribute of *Ia-ú-a* son of Omri, brought or sent about 841 BCE. Specialists generally identify him with Jehu of 2 Kings 9–10. “Son of Omri” is Assyria’s dynastic label for the kingdom of Israel. Jehu was not Omri’s biological son in 2 Kings.

### City of David, “Large Stone Structure”

There is no consensus that this is one 10th-century building, or David’s palace. Eilat Mazar dated remains to the 10th–9th centuries and compared them with 2 Samuel 5. Finkelstein, Herzog, Ussishkin, and Singer-Avitz, *Tel Aviv* 34 (2007), argued that the walls are not one building of that date. Dever, Amihai Mazar, Faust, and Magness have defended an Iron Age date. No inscription names David.

### Seen, and kept out of the secure list

- The Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon (Israel Museum; Misgav, Garfinkel, and Ganor, *Khirbet Qeiyafa* Vol. 1, 2009) is late 11th or early 10th century by radiocarbon. Galil, Puech, Millard, Demsky, and Rollston do not agree on the letters, the language, or whether it is prose, a name list, or a scribal exercise. It does not securely name anyone in Samuel.
- A Tell es-Safi/Gath sherd from a 10th- or early-9th-century context reads *ʾlwt* and *wlt*. Maeir treats the likeness to Goliath’s non-Semitic name (1 Samuel 17), and to Lydian Alyattes, as cultural consistency. It is not the name Goliath. A separate late-9th-century destruction and siege system is compared with Hazael’s capture of Gath in 2 Kings 12:18.
- The Samaria ostraca, found by Reisner in 1910 and published in *Harvard Excavations at Samaria, 1908–1910* (1924), are 8th-century delivery notes. No king is named. Year numbers are often linked to Jeroboam II (2 Kings 14). Clan place-names match Manasseh in Numbers 26 and Joshua 17. That is geography, not a Kings narrative.

## 701 BCE, the exile, and the Babylonian court

### Taylor Prism, BM 91032

Clay foundation prism from Nebi Yunus, registration 1855,1003.1, dated by the British Museum to 691 BCE under the eponym Bel-erumanni. Edition: Luckenbill, *The Annals of Sennacherib* (OIP 2, 1924), and RINAP 3. Sennacherib’s 701 campaign names Hezekiah of Judah, claims forty-six fortified cities and 200,150 people, and says Hezekiah was shut in Jerusalem like a caged bird. It does not claim that Jerusalem was captured. That matches the named king, the lost cities, and the city’s survival in 2 Kings 18–19 and Isaiah 36–37, including a later payment of 30 talents of gold. The prism’s 800 talents of silver do not match the 300 talents in 2 Kings 18:14. The angel of 2 Kings 19:35 is not in the Assyrian text. The 200,150 figure is what the prism claims.

### Lachish reliefs

Gypsum panels from Room XXXVI of Sennacherib’s South-West Palace at Nineveh. British Museum 124911 shows Sennacherib on a throne while the booty of Lachish passes before him. The museum dates the carving to 700–692 BCE and translates the caption: Sennacherib, king of the world, king of Assyria, set up a throne and the booty of Lachish passed before him. The checker rejected a “Layard, 1845–1847” discovery line: Layard uncovered the room in 1850, and Rassam removed the slabs in 1853. The museum record lists Rassam among the excavators. The caption’s reading “Lachish” is the accepted decipherment. A minority historical argument that the depicted city was Jerusalem is not a different reading of the signs. Read with the Taylor Prism, the two Assyrian sources agree with the biblical distinction: Lachish fell, Jerusalem did not. Ussishkin, *The Conquest of Lachish by Sennacherib* (1982), is the excavation report that ties the reliefs to the siege ramp at Tell ed-Duweir. The British Museum bibliography for the set includes Barnett, Bleibtreu, and Turner, *Sculptures from the Southwest Palace of Sennacherib at Nineveh* (1998).

### Siloam Tunnel inscription, KAI 189

Six lines of Paleo-Hebrew, found in 1880, now in the Istanbul Archaeology Museums. It describes the two digging crews meeting. It names no king. Christopher Rollston notes that most specialists connect it with Hezekiah’s conduit in 2 Kings 20:20 and 2 Chronicles 32:30. A minority date is later. Claims of extra lines naming Hezekiah were announced in 2022 without peer review. This stays a likely link, not a signed one.

### Fall of Samaria

Credit is split, and both sides are real inscriptions. The Babylonian Chronicle (Grayson, ABC 1) says Shalmaneser V ravaged Shamara’in, usually read as Samaria. Sargon II’s Khorsabad texts claim that he captured Samaria and deported 27,290 people. Second Kings 17:5–6 and 18:9–11 name Shalmaneser as the besieger and an unnamed king of Assyria as the one who took the city. Neither text proves the theological explanation in 2 Kings 17.

### Jerusalem Chronicle, BM 21946 (ABC 5)

Wiseman 1956; Grayson 1975. CDLI P555785. In Nebuchadnezzar’s seventh year, 598/597 BCE, he besieged the city of Judah, seized it on 2 Addaru, captured its king, appointed a king of his choosing, and took heavy tribute. The tablet does not name Jehoiachin or Zedekiah. Those names come from 2 Kings 24:10–17. The year agrees with Jeremiah 52:28 and conflicts with the eighth year in 2 Kings 24:12. The chronicle does not describe the burning of the Temple. No surviving chronicle covers the 587/586 destruction. The sequel tablet is lost.

### Jehoiachin ration tablets

Four Neo-Babylonian oil-ration tablets from the vaulted storehouse of Nebuchadnezzar’s South Palace at Babylon, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin. Ernst Weidner, “Jojachin, König von Juda, in babylonischen Keilschrifttexten,” *Mélanges Dussaud* II (1939) 923–935. The museum identifies VAT 16378 as an oil-ration list for prisoners at the court. They name Ya’u-kīnu, king of the land of Yahudu, and five sons. The preserved date is Nebuchadnezzar’s thirteenth year, 592/591 BCE. That is Jehoiachin in Babylon, still called king, after 597, as in 2 Kings 24:12–15. They do not record the later release under Amel-Marduk in 2 Kings 25:27–30.

### Lachish Letters

Paleo-Hebrew ostraca from the city-gate debris at Tell ed-Duweir, found by James Leslie Starkey in January–February 1935. Harry Torczyner, *Lachish I* (1938); KAI 192–199. About 590 BCE. Now in the British Museum (ME 125701–125707 and related numbers) and in Jerusalem. Letter 4 says the writer is watching Lachish’s signals because Azekah cannot be seen. That fits the military situation in Jeremiah 34:7, where Lachish and Azekah were the cities still holding. It does not name Jeremiah. Letter 3 mentions an unnamed prophet and Coniah son of Elnathan going to Egypt. Torczyner’s link to Uriah and Elnathan in Jeremiah 26:20–23 is disputed, because that episode is set under Jehoiakim, while the ostraca belong to the later, Zedekiah-period destruction layer.

### Belshazzar

Nabonidus foundation cylinder BM 91125 (K.1689), from Ur, dated by the British Museum to 556–539 BCE, names Belshazzar as Nabonidus’s son in a prayer to Sin. Rawlinson and Norris, *Cuneiform Inscriptions of Western Asia* I (1861), plate 68 no. 1, with duplicates K.1690–K.1692. That person and that relationship are secure. Daniel 5 calls him king and the son of Nebuchadnezzar and narrates a feast and a death on the night Babylon fell. The cylinder does not support that plot. The Nabonidus Chronicle (BM 35382; Grayson, ABC 7) records Nabonidus at Tema and Cyrus taking Babylon in year 17. The legible text used here does not name Belshazzar. Other Babylonian documents title him crown prince, not son of Nebuchadnezzar.

## Texts, temples, and transmission

### Ketef Hinnom KH1 and KH2

Silver plaques in Paleo-Hebrew, found in 1979 in the repository under Chamber 25 of Cave 24 in Jerusalem, Israel Museum. Barkay, Lundberg, Vaughn, and Zuckerman, *Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research* 334 (2004) 41–71, date them late pre-exilic, before 586/7 BCE. Renz and Roellig (1995) left a Hellenistic date open, and Nadav Na’aman (2011) argued for the early Second Temple period. The late pre-exilic date is the majority view of the 2004 edition, not a unanimous one. KH2 lines 5–12 are a shortened Priestly Blessing parallel to Numbers 6:24–26, lacking “be gracious to you” and “lift up his countenance.” They are the earliest known citations of wording that also stands in the Hebrew Bible. They do not prove that Numbers, or the Pentateuch, already existed as a book. KH1’s parallel to Deuteronomy 7:9 and Exodus 20:6 is more broken.

### Cyrus Cylinder, BM 90920

Baked clay, found by Hormuzd Rassam at Babylon in March 1879, written after Cyrus took the city in October 539 BCE. Translation followed here: Mordechai Cogan in Hallo and Younger, *The Context of Scripture*, vol. 2 (2003), adapted to Schaudig, as given on Livius. Cyrus says he returned gods and their people to Ashur, Susa, Agade, Eshnunna, Zamban, Me-Turnu, Der, and Gutium. That is the same kind of royal act as Ezra 1:1–4, Ezra 6:3–5, 2 Chronicles 36:22–23, and Isaiah 44:28–45:1. It is not those texts. The cylinder does not name Judah, Jerusalem, Yahweh, or Jews. It does not settle whether Ezra 1 and Ezra 6 preserve a genuine Persian edict. Kuhrt and Janzen were not re-read beyond summaries that report their caution.

### Elephantine

Berlin P. 13495 (TAD A4.7 = Cowley 30), from the 1907 Rubensohn excavation, edited by Porten and Yardeni. A draft of 20 Marheshvan, year 17 of Darius II (25 November 407 BCE), from Yedaniah and his colleagues to Bagavahya, governor of Judah. It asks permission to rebuild the temple of YHW at Elephantine, destroyed in year 14 (410 BCE), with meal-offering, incense, and holocaust. A Yahwistic temple stood in Egypt. That shows Yahweh-worship outside Jerusalem. It does not show that every Yahwist kept Deuteronomy 12’s single sanctuary.

The memorandum TAD A4.9 (Cowley 32; Berlin; after 407 BCE) records what Bagohi and Delaiah told a messenger to say before Arsames: rebuild the altar-house of the God of Heaven, which had stood before Cambyses and which Vidranga demolished in year 14 of Darius, and offer meal-offering and incense as formerly. As preserved it does not authorize the animal holocaust requested in TAD A4.7, and it does not record that the temple was rebuilt.

Berlin P. 13464 (TAD A4.1 = Cowley 21), year 5 of Darius II (419 BCE), is a fragmentary Aramaic letter from Hananiah to Yedaniah. Cowley’s restoration turned it into a royal order for Passover and Unleavened Bread, compared with Exodus 12:15–20 and Leviticus 23:6–8. The word Passover is not in the surviving text, the festival lines are heavily restored, and that identification is disputed. A 2024 argument by Barnea that the papyrus is not about Passover shows the old Cowley/Porten reading is not secure. It is not yet a replacement consensus. The papyrus does not securely prove an extrabiblical Passover in 419 BCE.

### Great Isaiah Scroll, 1QIsaa

Found in Qumran Cave 1 in 1947. Seventeen parchment sheets, 54 columns, about 734 cm, Shrine of the Book at the Israel Museum. Eugene Ulrich and Peter W. Flint, *Qumran Cave 1, II: The Isaiah Scrolls* (Discoveries in the Judaean Desert 32; Oxford: Clarendon, 2010). Palaeography dates the hand to about 125–100 BCE. Jull, Donahue, Broshi, and Tov, *Radiocarbon* 37 (1995), was not read, so no radiocarbon range is asserted. The scroll contains all 66 chapters of Hebrew Isaiah in Masoretic order and is generally close to the later Masoretic text, with more than 2,600 variants, including the absence of Isaiah 2:9b–10. Those missing words are treated as a later addition because they appear in other Qumran Isaiah copies, the Masoretic text, and the Septuagint. The other Cave 1 copy, 1QIsab, stands closer to the Masoretic text. This is evidence that a full Hebrew Isaiah was being copied in the Hasmonean period, and that the medieval text is not identical to this copy.

## Biblical counts

Magen Broshi and Israel Finkelstein, “The Population of Palestine in Iron Age II,” *Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research* 287 (1992) 47–60, are the scale check.

| Text | Figure | Against the population study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exodus 12:37; Numbers 1:46 | About 600,000 men on foot; 603,550 men. With women and children the camp is well over 2,000,000. | An adult-male total already larger than their estimate for the whole country centuries later. Not a credible Late Bronze or early Iron headcount. |
| Mid-8th century, their estimate | Western Palestine about 400,000. Judah about 110,000. Israel, including Gilead, up to about 350,000. | This is the scale of the kingdoms in the period when the inscriptions are densest. |
| 2 Samuel 24:9 | 800,000 Israelite and 500,000 Judahite fighting men. | The two census texts disagree with each other, and both exceed the later country-wide population. |
| 1 Chronicles 21:5 | 1,100,000 and 470,000. | Same problem. |

The mismatch limits the counts. It does not by itself produce a smaller historical Exodus. Colin Humphreys’s reduction of the census to about 5,500 men (*Vetus Testamentum* 48, 1998) is a contested proposal and is not adopted. Patriarchal ages in Genesis 5 and 11 are schematic, and the Masoretic, Samaritan, and Septuagint begetting figures are known to diverge, but no critical edition of those variants was inspected, so that point stays a warning rather than a sourced claim.

Assyrian totals have the same kind of problem from the other direction. Ancient royal headcounts, biblical and Assyrian, are a poor place to require a modern census.

## How the links work

The logical weight is convergence, not a single dramatic object.

From the 9th century BCE on, independent chanceries talk about the same small set of kingdoms the books of Kings talk about. A Moabite king names Omri, Israel, and YHWH. An Aramean monument names the House of David. Assyrian royal texts name Ahab at Qarqar, Jehu as the next generation of “the house of Omri,” and Hezekiah trapped in Jerusalem in 701. Babylonian palace bookkeeping names a king of Judah taken in 597 and Jehoiachin still called king in Babylon five years later. None of those archives depends on the Bible. A writer who invents a dynasty does not get confirmed by the enemy’s victory stele.

The fit is political, and it includes the disagreements. The prism and 2 Kings both have Hezekiah, the cities, the siege of Jerusalem, and a gold payment, and they disagree on the silver. The chronicle and Jeremiah agree on 597, and 2 Kings 24:12 says the eighth year. Mesha’s campaign is not a copy of 2 Kings 3. That pattern is what you expect when two sides record one war.

The early period does not have that chain. Merneptah shows that the name Israel was already in use for a people in Canaan. It does not show where they came from. Et-Tell does not have the city Joshua 8 says was burned. Jericho’s excavated sequence has not been shown to be Joshua 6.

Copying is a different question from origins. Ketef Hinnom shows Priestly Blessing wording on silver before the exile, in a shorter form. The Great Isaiah Scroll shows a complete Hebrew Isaiah in the late 2nd century BCE, close to the medieval text and not identical with it. That is evidence of transmission. It is not evidence that Isaiah’s book, or the Pentateuch, was written on the dates tradition assigns.

What archaeology can test is public names, places, offices, and events that leave stone, clay, or a mound. It cannot test a private conversation or a theological claim. A match of name and decade is evidence of historical memory. It is not a transcript.

## New Testament

### Pilate stone

Israel Museum, IAA 1961-529. Hard limestone, found reused in a staircase of the theater at Caesarea. The museum dates it 26–36 CE and calls it the only object from his time that bears his name. The surviving lines include a Tiberieum, Pontius Pilate, and prefect of Judea. The left side and the closing verb are restored. The secure facts are the name and the prefect title. The museum’s catalog title still says “procurator,” which is the older literary title. The stone itself supports prefect. Compared with Matthew 27, Luke 3:1, and John 18–19, it corroborates the official. It does not corroborate the trial. Alföldy’s lighthouse reading is widely followed and is not the only restoration of the broken lines.

Catalog: https://www.imj.org.il/en/collections/395572-0

### Caiaphas ossuary

Israel Museum, IAA 1991-468, from a tomb at Talpiot found in 1990. Soft limestone, 1st century CE. “Joseph son of Caiaphas” is written twice on the most ornate of twelve ossuaries. The museum infers that this is the high priest of Matthew 26:57 from the name, the decoration, and the date. The box does not say “high priest.” The name was not unique. The identification is probable. It is an inference, not a title read off the stone.

Catalog: https://www.imj.org.il/en/collections/191542-0

### Gallio at Delphi

Nine fragments survive of a Greek copy of a letter of Claudius. The edition is André Plassart, *Fouilles de Delphes* III.4.3 no. 286 (1970, pp. 27–32), also SIG³ 801 D. A fragment with the name is in the Delphi museum. No inventory number was verified. The Epigraphische Datenbank Heidelberg record HD043294 could not be opened. Plassart’s physical description of the block was not opened, so it is not asserted that the fragments are the Saint-Élie limestone orthostates, although no. 286 is in that fascicle.

The line on the stone reads νιος Γαλλίων ὁ φ[ίλος] μου κα[ὶ ἀνθύ]πατος. Γαλλίων, ὁ, and μου are preserved. In φίλος only a dotted phi is read. In ἀνθύπατος, ἀνθύ is restored and only πατος is read, with the pi dotted. Plassart’s text does not include τῆς Ἀχαΐας. That province name was an earlier restoration.

The only numeral preserved on the stone is κϛ´, the 26th imperatorial acclamation. The 12th tribunician year is a restoration. The letter is not dated by a tribunician number actually read on the stone. A claim that the letter belongs to the first half of 52, after about 25 January, was rejected. The 26th acclamation is earlier than the 1 August 52 dedication that attests the 27th. The exact day is unknown. The consuls Sulla and Titianus fix the year 52. Frontinus’s *ab urbe condita* numeral is textually unstable and was not used.

The preserved [Ἰού]νιος Γαλλίων is accepted as L. Junius Gallio Annaeanus. A proconsulship of Achaia in 51/52 is only provisional. The title is mostly restored, the province is not in Plassart’s text, and Seneca says Gallio left Achaia when a fever began (*Epistulae* 104.1). Plassart took the addressee to be Gallio’s successor. James H. Oliver, *Hesperia* 40 (1971) 239–240, heads the letter in 52 and argues that Gallio was still proconsul when Claudius wrote.

The letter is about Delphi, the cult of Pythian Apollo, and new settlers. Gallio appears in a report that the city had lost citizens. It does not mention Paul, Corinth, a hearing, or a judgment.

PHI text: https://epigraphy.packhum.org/text/240470

### Erastus pavement

Corinth VIII.3 no. 232, as edited, reads “[praenomen nomen] Erastus pro aedilit[at]e / s(ua) p(ecunia) stravit.” A man with the cognomen Erastus paved something at his own expense in return for an aedileship. The praenomen, the nomen, and the letters AT are restorations. The edition is headed in Nero’s reign. Kent’s 1966 pages were not opened. His Nero heading and his acceptance of the identification are taken from PHI and from quotations in Friesen and Gill.

The American School found the main slab on 15 April 1929, in the paving where a street northeast of the theatre enters a square. A piece of the right-hand slab was found in 1928 in the east parodos, and the rest in 1947 southwest of the theatre. The pieces were later set in that plaza. Henry J. Cadbury, “Erastus of Corinth,” *Journal of Biblical Literature* 50 (1931).

In 1929 the preserved text was ERASTVS PRO AED / S P STRAVIT. AED was expanded as *aedilitate*. After the right-hand fragments, the edition reads *pro aedilit[at]e*, so only AT is restored inside a word that is otherwise there. The stone does not contain the standalone noun *aedilis*. The aedileship formula is the standard reading.

Kent’s date near the middle of the first century rested on identifying the donor with Paul’s Erastus, so that date cannot be used to prove the identification. A mid-second-century date, toward the end of Hadrian’s reign or later, is known here only as Steven Friesen’s report of a personal communication from Charles Williams: the pavement covers a latrine used until the Hadrianic period. That excavation report was not opened.

Romans 16:23 (SBL Greek New Testament) says Ἔραστος ὁ οἰκονόμος τῆς πόλεως, Erastus the steward of the city, and Quartus the brother. The usual Greek for Latin *aedilis* is *agoranomos*, which this verse does not use. Equating *oikonomos* of the city with the Corinthian aedileship has not been shown. Goodrich defends a municipal quaestorship for the Pauline *oikonomos* and, in the passage inspected, declines to decide whether that man is the Corinthian aedile.

The pavement text is accepted. The identification with Romans 16:23 is disputed. Kent thought it still sound, and others accept it. Friesen, “The Wrong Erastus,” in *Corinth in Context* (Brill, 2010), argues it is the wrong man. Meggitt and Friesen also leave [Ep]erastus open, because the left side is missing and the centering of the second line is part of the argument. PHI prints Erastus. The stone does not corroborate the greeting.

Cadbury: https://www.biblicalstudies.org.uk/pdf/jbl/1931_042.pdf

Friesen: https://brill.com/downloadpdf/book/edcoll/9789004190610/Bej.9789004181977.i-506_009.pdf

### Bethesda

In July 1888, monks digging northwest of St Anne’s Church found a rock-cut tank, and Conrad Schick reported a second pool to its west. The Palestine Exploration Fund’s Notes and News called this the Pool of Bethesda and set aside Birket Israil and the twin pools at the Sisters of Zion. *Palestine Exploration Fund Quarterly Statement*, July 1888, pp. 111–112.

That notice did not report five excavated colonnades. It inferred five porticoes from the twin-pool plan: one along each outer side and one on the dividing wall. Shimon Gibson’s 2011 report, read here only as quoted by Ferrell Jenkins, still describes the five porticoes as a reconstruction: the northern reservoir and the southern bathing pool “would have been” surrounded by four porticoes plus one across the barrier wall. Gibson’s chapter in *Sainte-Anne de Jérusalem* (Proche-Orient Chrétien, 2011) was not opened.

A published account of the White Fathers’ excavations (Guy Couturier, “Bethesda,” InterBible, 15 February 2008, drawing on *Parabole* x/2, 1987) says the two large trapezoidal basins, about 13 m deep and separated by a 6.5 m division, were found and dated to the second century BCE. Remains of porticoes around those basins and on the central division were not identified. Pierre Benoit’s 1968 report, often cited for the same negative point, was not opened and is not used as a claim. A caption that calls an object a capital from the portico, without a date or find context, does not show five in-situ colonnades.

The St Anne double pool is the working identification of the pool in John 5. The healing is not an archaeological result. A healing cult on the site is a later or adjacent installation: Hadrian’s temple of Asclepius and Serapis, and at least one eastern cave bath assigned to Fortuna rather than Asclepius.

The name moves with the manuscripts and stays in Jerusalem. Beth-zatha is tied to the northern quarter Bezetha. Bethsaida is treated as a corruption of the Jerusalem name, not as the Galilean town. Bethesda has been defended from the Copper Scroll’s Bet-eshdatain as a name for a two-basin pool. That connection is an interpretation. The Copper Scroll does not name John’s pool. A critical edition was not checked, so no claim is made that NA28 prefers Bethzatha.

### Siloam

Reich and Shukron found a large stepped pool at Birkat el-Ḥamra and identified it as the late Second Temple Siloam Pool. That is a different pool from the smaller one at the outlet of the Siloam Tunnel. The smaller pool is the one earlier scholarship had called Siloam. It was afterward dated to the Late Roman–Byzantine period, incorporated into the Byzantine church, and it lies less than 200 yards from a reconstruction built between 400 and 460 by the Empress Eudocia.

The stepped pool was found in 2004 during repair of a sewer line, under Eli Shukron, with Ronny Reich. It had been announced by 23 December 2004. The month is not exact: that report says “four months ago,” and a 9 August 2005 report says “last fall.”

The exposed pool had three flights of five steps separated by narrow landings, with steps on three sides. Reich’s 2021 summary of the 1995–2010 City of David excavations describes it as entirely stone-lined, plastered in its first phase like ritual baths, and identifies it with the Siloam Pool of John 9. The PDF text extraction is OCR-damaged. The John 9 identification and the plaster-then-stone description are readable. *Qadmoniot* 130 (2005) 91–96 and the full 2011 chapter in *Unearthing Jerusalem* (pp. 241–255) were not read as claims. The De Gruyter HTML page displays the opening prose of that chapter. Coin dates and dimensions sometimes quoted from it are not claimed here. Four Alexander Jannaeus plaster coins and a dozen revolt coins in one corner are reported. Those reports do not date abandonment to during the First Revolt.

The identification is provisional. Reich still equates the stepped pool with John 9. Nahshon Szanton, *‘Atiqot* 113 (2023), assigns the Siloam of the textual sources, including John 9:7, to the smaller Early Roman pool at the tunnel outlet, and identifies Birkat el-Ḥamra as Josephus’s Solomon’s Pool. His article is a published argument by an Israel Antiquities Authority archaeologist. It is not a field-wide rejection of Reich. Neither argument treats either pool as proof of the healing.

### Hezekiah bulla

Hebrew University release, 2 December 2015, Ophel excavations directed by Eilat Mazar. A clay sealing from a refuse dump, found with 33 other bullae, reads “Belonging to Hezekiah [son of] Ahaz king of Judah,” with a two-winged sun and two ankh signs. Measurements in the release: impression about 9.7 by 8.6 mm on a bulla about 13 by 12 mm. Mazar reported it as the first seal impression of an Israelite or Judean king from a scientific excavation. Sealings of Hezekiah from the antiquities market were already known. The reading of this excavated impression is strong. The same release dates the adjacent building to the second half of the 10th century and calls it Solomon’s government quarter. That building date is the excavator’s interpretation and sits inside the disputed 10th-century question above. It is not established by the bulla. This item rests on the excavator’s press release, not on a journal article opened in these passes.

## Still unmarked

No excavation report, catalog, or corpus entry for Nazareth, Capernaum, or the Herodian monuments survived verification, so none of these receives a grade.

- The Cyrus Cylinder as the decree in Ezra.
